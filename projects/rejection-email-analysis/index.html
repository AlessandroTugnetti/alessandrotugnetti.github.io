After Clicking Apply

For months, I read each rejection email and saved it in a folder. As the folder grew, I wondered what the messages could tell me when I read them together. Do rejections come in waves? How often does an employer say why they turned me down?

What I found

When I get a rejection, the median wait is about four weeks. At 90 days, two in five older applications still have no reply. Even when I do hear back, most emails either give no reason or say the employer is moving ahead with other candidates.

Scope and method

I have been applying for jobs exclusively in Luxembourg since November 2025. I apply through company careers pages whenever I can and use LinkedIn for roles that are only listed there. I try to apply as soon as I spot a role, usually within a week of the posting date. I update this analysis as I go. The figures here are current as of November 6, 2026.

The timeline

When the rejections come in

I start with the dates the rejection emails arrive. Do applications and rejections peak in the same months, or does the busiest period in my inbox come later?

Across the search, I count 318 applications and 159 rejections. I send the most applications in July and record the most rejections in October. A rejection in October can come from an application I sent weeks earlier.

The replies

How far I get

I count a reply with a known date for 95 applications (29.9%). For 17 applications (5.3%), I receive an invitation or request to take a next step.

Sometimes the next step is a test or a call before a formal interview. I attend interviews for 9 applications (2.8%).

I receive a rejection after the interview for four of those nine applications. Rafa Audibert groups recruiter calls with first interviews in his remote engineering search. I keep interviews separate so I can see how often I get past the earlier steps. Audibert’s search

The wait

How long I wait

The median wait for a first reply is 18 days. Six of the 95 first replies (6%) arrive the day I apply. Twelve (13%) take 90 days or more.

For rejections, I wait a median of 26 days. Four of the 102 rejections I can time (4%) arrive the same day, and 18 (18%) take at least 90 days. An automated decision could explain some of the quickest rejections, but I cannot tell from the timing alone.

Thomas Rones uses confirmation emails as his starting dates and gets a five-day median for applications he can pair with a reply. I count from the date I apply, so I can include a rejection that arrives without an archived confirmation. Rones’s job-search analysis

The silence

When I hear nothing

When I hear nothing after applying, I wonder whether I am still in the running. I look at how many applications remain open as the weeks pass. I am surprised by how many are still open after three months. I have no reply or outcome for 70 of the 175 applications at least 90 days old, or 40%.

How long do I wait before I call it ghosting? Loïc Sans uses two months in his own search. I check at several points because a late reply can still come. One rejection arrives 193 days after I apply. Sans’s job-search log

The timestamp

When the rejections land

My job search goes back to November 2025. The 80 rejection emails I look at here come from a shorter stretch, from August to early November.

Thursday is the busiest day in my inbox. I count 19 emails in the 10 a.m. hour and six on weekends. Conor Dewey’s analysis puts Wednesday first and also finds some rejections on Saturdays. Job Rejection Analysis notebook

The wording

Have I read this before?

I start with a phrase I recognize. In his job rejection analysis, Conor Dewey finds that some version of “thank you for your interest” is the most common subject line. I look in the body of the emails and find a thank-you in 52 of 53 English messages. Dewey’s analysis

Across the sample, I count 35 emails that give no reason and 26 that point to other candidates. Together they make up 76% of my sample. Liz Dugan puts roughly 73% of her emails in those same broad categories, with other candidates first. We classify the emails differently, but I recognize the pattern in her results. Most of the time the letter tells me the outcome and leaves me guessing what I could have done differently.

Dugan compares how long emails are when they use certain phrases. I want to see how much of the wording repeats beyond the phrases I recognize. For each email, I look for the closest match in the same language. The chart shows how many emails fall in each score range. A score of 0 means the two emails share no three-word sequences. At 1, the sets of sequences are identical. I count scores at or above the dashed line at 0.75 as close matches.

At my cutoff, 36 emails have a close match. For 12 of those emails, at least one close match comes from a different recorded employer. I recognize the format much more often than I can match the wording across employers.

Jablin and Krone describe a recurring structure in post-screening rejection letters. Barešová finds that 86% of the American letters she studies open with appreciation. I count thanks wherever they appear in my English emails. That familiar gesture gives the letters a recognizable shape even when the wording varies. Jablin and Krone, Barešová

The jobs

Does the role make a difference?

My skills and qualifications lead me mainly toward data and analytics, so technical roles make up the largest group in my search, with 117 applications. I count 79 in finance and investment and 6 in compliance and financial crime. One investment risk role also mentions data integration, so I count it in both groups.

I count 60 rejections among those 117 data and technical applications, and 41 among the 79 finance and investment applications. I also have applications still waiting for an outcome in both groups. The chart shows where I send most of my applications and where the rejections I know about accumulate.

I also wonder whether larger employers take longer to send a rejection or word their emails differently. I compare waits for employers with more than 1,000 staff and those with fewer than 250, using published headcounts for the company or its group. I have too few emails with verified headcounts to compare wording by employer size.
