After I Click Apply

I used to read a rejection email and save it in a folder. After enough of those emails I started to wonder what I missed by reading them only once. Do they come in waves? And how often does one tell me why I get a no?

I update this analysis as the search continues, and the numbers here are current as of November 5, 2026.

The timeline

When the rejections come in

I start applying for jobs in November 2025 and I’m still looking. The emails that come back are one of the few parts of the search I can revisit. The calendar gives me a place to start. Do I hear back in the months when I send the most applications?

Across the whole search I count 317 applications and 157 rejections. I can put 84 of those rejections on the calendar. An application can disappear from view for weeks before it shows up again as a reply.

The replies

How far I get

I can date a reply for 94 applications (29.7%). In 16 cases (5.0%), I have a next step such as a test or an interview.

I attend interviews for 9 applications (2.8%). One process has a screening call on the calendar as well as an interview. A scheduled call or a request for a test can be a first step well before I reach an interview.

I have a rejection on record for 4 of those 9 applications. Rafa Audibert groups recruiter calls with first interviews in his remote engineering search. I keep interviews separate so I can see how often I get past the earlier steps. Audibert’s search

The wait

How long I wait

I wait about three weeks for a first reply. The median is 19.5 days, and for a rejection it’s 28.5. The chart has replies on the day I apply and rejections that take months.

Thomas Rones uses confirmation emails as his starting dates and gets a five-day median for applications he can pair with a reply. I start from my submission dates, so I can include a rejection that arrives without an archived confirmation. Rones’s job-search analysis

The silence

When I hear nothing

The first thing I get after applying is a receipt. What I want to know is whether I hear anything after that. For 70 of the 175 applications at least 90 days old, I still have no reply or outcome. That’s 40%.

How long does that blank have to last before I call it ghosting? Loïc Sans uses two months in his own ongoing search. I look at several points on the calendar. With a rejection arriving on day 136, two months feels too early to call every quiet application finished. Sans’s job-search log

The timestamp

When the rejections land

When a rejection does land, I can see when it reaches me. I have 79 rejection emails from August to early November.

Thursday is the busiest day in my inbox. I count 18 emails in the 10 a.m. hour and six on weekends. In the notebook that first made me look at the timestamps, Wednesday is the busiest day and Saturday shows up there too. These are delivery times. A timestamp tells me when I can read the message. The wording

Have I read this before?

The same notebook finds that a variation on “thank you for your interest” is the most common subject line. I look for thanks in the body and find it in 52 of 53 English emails. A thank-you is easy to spot. A reason I can do something with takes longer to find.

Across the four languages, I count 35 emails that give no reason and 26 that point to other candidates. Together they make up 77% of my sample. Liz Dugan puts roughly 73% of her emails in those same broad categories, with other candidates first. We classify the emails differently, but I recognize the pattern in her results. Most of the time the letter tells me the outcome and leaves me guessing what I could have done differently.

Dugan compares email length across common phrases. I’m curious about a different question. If I take out the names and signatures, how close is one whole email to another? I look for the closest match in the same language.

At my cutoff, 36 emails have a close match. For 12 of those emails, at least one close match comes from a different recorded employer. I recognize the format much more often than I can match the wording across employers.

Jablin and Krone describe a recurring structure in post-screening rejection letters. Barešová finds that 86% of the American letters she studies open with appreciation. I count thanks wherever they appear in my English emails. That familiar gesture gives the letters a recognizable shape even when the wording varies. Jablin and Krone, Barešová

The jobs

Does the role make a difference?

Data and technical titles are the largest group in my search, with 117 applications. I count 79 in finance and investment and just 6 in compliance and financial crime. The titles can cross over. One investment risk role also mentions data integration, so I count it in both groups.

I have 58 rejections on record among those 117 data and technical applications, and 41 among the 79 finance and investment applications. I also have applications still waiting for an outcome in both groups. The chart shows where I send most of my applications and where the rejections I know about accumulate.

I also look at employer size. I can verify headcounts for nine employers, which cover 39 of my applications. The two rejection waits I can measure for smaller employers both come from one company. In the larger group I can follow more of the replies, but even there the measured waits come from a handful of applications.

When I get a rejection, the median wait is about four weeks. At 90 days, two in five older applications still have no reply. Even when I do hear back, most emails either give no reason or say the employer is moving ahead with other candidates. That is what I miss when I read each email only once. I can see how long the search stays open and how rarely a rejection tells me what to try next.

Scope and method

The monthly chart counts applications and rejections in the months they occur.

The emails used for text and arrival patterns are a subset of the correspondence. Application outcomes come from a broader record. The timing calculations use calendar dates and omit estimated rejection dates. Interview rescheduling and reminders are counted once for the same appointment.

The closest-match score is the size of the intersection of two sets of distinct three-word sequences divided by the size of their union. The 0.75 threshold is a descriptive choice. Messages are matched within language, with identifying text masked where the analysis can recognize it.
