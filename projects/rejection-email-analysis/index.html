After Clicking Apply

I used to read a rejection email and save it in a folder. After enough of those emails I started to wonder what I missed by reading them only once. Do they come in waves? And how often does one tell me why I get a no?

What I found

The median wait for a rejection is about four weeks. Two in five applications that are at least 90 days old still have no reply. Most rejection emails give no reason or simply say the employer is moving ahead with other candidates.

Scope and method

I’ve been applying for jobs since November 2025, all in Luxembourg. I apply through company careers pages when I can and use LinkedIn for jobs listed only there. I try to apply as soon as I see a posting, usually within a week of it going live. I update this analysis as I go. The figures here are current as of November 5, 2026.

The timeline

When the rejections come in

When I send a batch of applications, I wonder if the rejections will come back in a batch too. Do I hear back in the months when I apply the most?

I count 317 applications and 157 rejections. I have dates for 84 of those rejections. A rejection can arrive weeks after I apply, so a busy month in my inbox can have more to do with earlier applications.

The replies

How far I get

I have a dated reply for 94 applications (29.7%). For 16 (5.0%), I get a next step, such as a test or an interview.

I attend interviews for 9 applications (2.8%). For one of those jobs, I have both a screening call and an interview on the calendar. A call or a test can be the first step in the process, long before an interview.

I have a rejection on record for 4 of those 9 applications. In his remote engineering search, Rafa Audibert counts recruiter calls alongside first interviews. I count interviews separately to see how often I make it that far. Audibert’s search

The wait

How long I wait

I wait about three weeks for a first reply. The median is 19.5 days, and for a rejection it’s 28.5. Some replies arrive the day I apply. Some rejections take months.

Thomas Rones measures from the confirmation email and finds a five-day median for applications he can match to a reply. I count from the day I apply, so I can include rejections even when I don’t have the confirmation email. Rones’s job-search analysis

The silence

When I hear nothing

I get an automatic confirmation and then I wait. For 70 of the 175 applications that are at least 90 days old, I still have no reply or outcome. That’s 40%.

How long do I wait before I call it ghosting? Loïc Sans uses two months in his own search. I check at several points because I get one rejection on day 136. Two months feels early to write off every application I haven’t heard back about. Sans’s job-search log

The timestamp

When the rejections land

I use 79 rejection emails from August to early November to look at when they arrive and what they say. For application outcomes, I use my full record going back to November 2025.

I get the most rejections on Thursdays. I count 18 emails in the 10 a.m. hour and six on weekends. In the notebook that prompts me to look at delivery times, Wednesday comes first and some emails arrive on Saturdays. Job Rejection Analysis notebook

The wording

Have I read this before?

The same notebook finds that a variation on “thank you for your interest” is the most common subject line. I look for thanks in the body and find it in 52 of 53 English emails. A thank-you is easy to spot. A reason I can do something with takes longer to find.

Across the four languages, I count 35 emails that give no reason and 26 that point to other candidates. Together they make up 77% of my sample. Liz Dugan puts roughly 73% of her emails in those same broad categories, with other candidates first. We classify the emails differently, but I recognize the pattern in her results. Most of the time the letter tells me the outcome and leaves me guessing what I could have done differently.

Dugan compares the length of emails that use common phrases. I want to know how much of the wording repeats. If I take out the names and signatures, how close is one email to another? I look for the closest match in the same language.

At my cutoff, 36 emails have a close match. For 12 of those emails, at least one close match comes from a different recorded employer. I recognize the format much more often than I can match the wording across employers.

Jablin and Krone describe a recurring structure in post-screening rejection letters. Barešová finds that 86% of the American letters she studies open with appreciation. I count thanks wherever they appear in my English emails. That familiar gesture gives the letters a recognizable shape even when the wording varies. Jablin and Krone, Barešová

The jobs

Does the role make a difference?

I apply most often to data and technical roles, with 117 applications. I count 79 in finance and investment and just 6 in compliance and financial crime. One investment risk job also mentions data integration, so I count it in both groups.

I count 58 rejections from 117 data and technical applications, and 41 from 79 finance and investment applications. I’m still waiting to hear back on some applications in both groups.

If you’re sending out applications and wondering whether everyone else is hearing back, I wrote this for you. I’m still waiting on replies, too, and I’ll keep applying. Hang in there.
